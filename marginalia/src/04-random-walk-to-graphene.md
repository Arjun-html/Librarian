Professor Andre Geim gave a public talk at The University of Hong Kong about his journey with graphene, the allotrope of carbon he discovered in the early 2000s.

He started his tenure-ship with a series of experiments focused on curiosity. His first were on the "magnetic water" phenomenon — easily explained away with the diamagnetism of water, but nevertheless made for some engaging visuals and promoted science. His image of suspending a live frog in a sphere of water was striking enough to be put into textbooks around the world, while earning him the infamous Ig Nobel.

![Live frog levitating in a 16-tesla magnetic field — the 1997 image that earned Geim the Ig Nobel](images/levitating-frog.jpg)

Loose notes from the second half of the talk, to expand later:

- Klein tunnelling

- Graphene applications: dissolve electronics and filter through graphene to get gold; 2D crystals; mix-and-match stacking; monolayer assembly; VdW heterostructures; batteries
